Measured against a real ledger, 2026-09-10.

--- what the two numbers are, and are not -------------------------------------

The false-match rate rests on receivables this project did not create — the accounting sandbox
ships them, with update dates going back to 2008. The two populations were separated by asking
the provider which invoices this application created, not by a heuristic over dates or names.
Voided, deleted and draft invoices are excluded: a voided number can be reissued, and a draft
may carry no number at all, so both would produce artifacts of the query rather than of the
matcher.

The false-non-match figure is NOT a rate, and is not published as one. A pair that must match
is the same invoice as a second lender would transcribe it, and there is no second lender. Six
renderings were exercised; five are ours, each with a documented reason. Exactly one was
produced by the ledger rather than by us — the same invoice carries a net line of 250,000.00
and 275,000.00 owed — and all six survive the threshold.

--- the one false match, opened rather than counted ---------------------------

At the chosen threshold there is exactly one, and it is not a defect in the matcher:

  ORC1003   Hamilton Smith Pty   550.00 AUD   due 2026-06-19
  ORC1008   Hamilton Smith Pty   550.00 AUD   due 2026-06-19

Two real invoices to one debtor, identical in amount and due date, differing only in their
number. They agree on six of seven because they genuinely are near-identical claims. This is
also the shape of the near-collision squatting attack: an adversary constructing a claim to
collide with someone else's would build exactly this. It is closed by debtor confirmation and
by the atomic binding of a lien to its disbursement, not by the threshold.

--- what the ledger's shape does to this --------------------------------------

Three of the seven components are constant here: one currency, one country, one issuer. Every
pair starts at three of seven before anything about the debt is compared. That is not a flaw in
the measurement, it is the measurement: a single-ledger deployment has four discriminating
components, and this is what four buys.

The amount bucket is nearly a fourth constant. Real amounts run from 105.60 to 7,150.00 and
occupy seven doubling buckets, with 19 of 43 claims in one of them. A uniform generator would
have hidden that; real data did not.

Almost every debtor is identified by name rather than by a tax number — the ledger carries a
tax number for one contact out of eighty-three. A name discriminates worse than a tax number,
so these figures are pessimistic against a deployment where debtors are identified fiscally.
That is the correct direction to be wrong in.

--- what the projection said, and what the measurement said -------------------

Before any of this ran, grouping the ledger's invoices by debtor, bucket and due date projected
one false match at the chosen threshold and sixty-nine one step down. The measurement gives one
and forty-seven. The first was right; the second was not, because the projection grouped by
contact and by raw amount while the measurement groups by canonical components with the name
fallback and the status filter. The measurement is what stands.

--- honest limits ---------------------------------------------------------------

Every figure above is an interval, not a point. A single false match in nine hundred pairs
reads as 0.11%, and the honest bound on it is roughly five times that. Zero matches would not
have meant zero either — it would have meant under a third of a percent.

More invoices would not sharpen this. Rows from one generator correlate with one another, so
ten thousand synthetic invoices do not buy ten thousand independent negatives. The invoices
this ledger shipped with are the ceiling of what the sandbox can honestly supply.

A commercial requirement quoted in the literature — at most ten false matches, a one percent
false-non-match rate — is recorded as precedent for the METHOD: fix targets from practice and
calibrate against a corpus. It is not used to judge these figures. Its "ten false matches"
carries no denominator, and it describes a biometric system over databases a billion rows deep.
