Escrow behaviour and cost, measured against the real registry, pool and USDC on Arc Testnet.

--- what this contract is for ---------------------------------------------------

In factoring the debtor owes the face value of their invoice, and the financier is owed the
advance plus a fee. Those are different numbers, and only the second is on chain. The escrow is
where the first becomes the second: the debtor pays what they owe, in one payment or several,
and settlement hands the pool its number and returns the difference to the business.

Nothing here holds discretion. Paying credits a lien, settling is arithmetic anyone can check,
and the contract never keeps a balance that is not attributed to a lien and to the payer who
sent it.

--- the split, measured ---------------------------------------------------------

One advance of 250.000000 USDC at 150 basis points, funded from a pool of 500, with the debtor
paying 300 — the face value they owe, which is not the financier's number:

    pool receives        253.750000 USDC      the advance plus its flat fee
    business receives     46.250000 USDC      the surplus, returned
    escrow retains         0.000000 USDC      asserted, not assumed

Exactly the amount due settles too, and returns nothing to the business. Below it, settlement
reverts carrying both numbers — what was paid and what is owed — rather than a bare failure.

The allowance the escrow grants the pool is set back to zero explicitly after `repay` returns,
and that zero is asserted. Today `repay` happens to pull exactly what was approved; the day it
pulls less, a live allowance would otherwise survive.

--- measured gas, receipt level -------------------------------------------------

    pay                  105,307 gas     $0.0021
    settle               131,753 gas     $0.0026
    refund                65,260 gas     $0.0013
    deployment           805,182 gas     3,436 bytes of runtime, 21,140 of margin

A debtor's whole journey — one payment and the settlement — is 237,060 gas, about half a cent at
the 20 Gwei floor. Both figures sit inside their budgets of 110,000 and 200,000.

--- money only enters where it can leave, and leaves where it cannot ------------

Settlement is the way this contract passes money on, and it needs a lien that is active and not
yet settled. So those are exactly the conditions under which a payment is accepted. A released
lien, a defaulted one, one that does not exist, or one already settled is refused at the door,
with the status inside the error.

That rule replaced an earlier design with a second exit for late payments. Building it showed why
it was wrong: it paid the vault money the vault had never lent, it could be sequenced ahead of
the loss write-down to inflate the share price by half and be redeemed in the same transaction,
and its premise was false — the vault counts its own balance, so a plain transfer already
recovers value for anyone who wants to send one.

But the door rule is checked on the way in and can be revoked on the way out, and that would
destroy money nobody mishandled. Three ordinary events close the exit after a payment is already
inside:

  a default report arrives after a partial payment;
  a release report arrives for the same reason;
  the business calls the pool's `repay` itself, which is permissionless by design and is an
  honest thing to do — and leaves the escrow holding a payment with nowhere to go.

None needs an attacker or a bug. So each payer's own contribution is tracked separately, and
`refund` returns it to them once settlement is impossible: the lien is no longer active, or the
pool's principal for it is already gone. It opens nowhere else. While settlement is still on the
table a payment stays, because it is going to be handed on.

This is not the shape that was rejected. It moves a payer's own money back to that payer: it
never touches the vault's assets, so it cannot be sequenced against a write-down, and it can
never pay out an advance the vault did not make. Each of the three guards was proven to
discriminate by mutation — removing the settled check, opening the refund unconditionally, and
paying out the aggregate instead of the payer's share each turn a different test red, the last
handing one payer 140.000000 USDC where 100.000000 was theirs.

--- what it does not do ---------------------------------------------------------

It does not hide the financing terms from the debtor. `registry.lienOf(lienId)` is a public view
returning the advance and the rate to anyone holding the identifier, and no contract can take
that back. What the escrow removes is the NEED for the debtor to know them: they pay their own
number, not the financier's.

It does not decide when an advance is released. It emits `Settled`, and a workflow watching that
event produces the signed report that moves the lien to released. Until that workflow exists, a
settled advance is paid in full and still reads as encumbered — asserted in a test, so the gap is
recorded rather than assumed — and, because it is marked settled here, it refuses further
payments rather than swallowing them.

It does not know that a payment arrived late. A debtor paying after a default gets their money
back through `refund`; deciding that the payment should instead have settled the advance needs a
system that knows when it arrived, which is the workflow's knowledge and not a status byte's.

It cannot help if Circle blocks this address. The blocklist runs below the EVM, so a blocked
escrow can neither pay out nor refund, and no code here changes that.
