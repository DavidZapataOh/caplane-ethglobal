The custody chain, link by link, and what preemptive poisoning actually costs.

--- the four links, and who can check each one ---------------------------------

  ClaimSubmitted(submissionId, submitter, ciphertext)
      the id is keccak256(submitter, ciphertext), enforced by the contract, so the identifier
      cannot belong to anyone but its sender
        |
  Lien{submissionId, borrower}
      asserted by the enclave. The registry stores the identifier without looking at it
        |
  Disbursed(lienId, borrower, advance)
      the pool reads borrower and amount from the registry, never from its caller
        |
  Settled(lienId, toPool, toBorrower)
      three public views recompute the split

Two of the four are enforced by contract. The middle one is not, and this file is about what
that means.

--- what changed, and what deliberately did not ---------------------------------

The inbox now records who submitted, in the same word that already recorded when: a uint96 block
number beside a 160-bit address. Measured by receipt, on an Osaka node:

    submit, 275-byte envelope     53,935  ->  54,144 gas     +209
    submit, 4,096-byte envelope  186,980  ->  186,980 gas       +0
    cheapest valid submission     46,983  ->   47,192 gas     +209
    storage per submission              one slot, unchanged

The plan this came from predicted +164 at the real size and a 361-gas SAVING at the cap. Both
were wrong, and the second could not have been right: at the cap the charge is the EIP-7623
calldata floor, which is a function of the calldata alone. It absorbs the execution cost
entirely, so no change to this contract's execution can move that number in either direction.
+209 is the honest figure, and it is what an extra address costs to pack, not what a slot costs:
an SSTORE from zero prices the slot and not its contents, and the slot was already being paid
for.

Duplicate detection now keys on the submitter rather than the block number. At block zero the
old check accepted the same submission twice and silently overwrote the record — asserted in a
test, and the test goes red if the check moves back. Neither half is infallible: a submission
pranked from the zero address would defeat this one. The submitter is the half a real
transaction always fills in, and the change was free.

No contract enforces that a lien's borrower is that submitter, and that is a decision rather than
an omission. The forwarder only relays reports the DON signed after verifying an enclave
attestation, so no outsider can mint one; the only reports that could name the wrong borrower are
our own, malformed. A bug belongs in front of its own test, not behind a cold storage read on the
money path forever — and a guard on the payment would not have stopped the bad lien from being
recorded anyway, only from being paid.

So the comparison is the workflow's, where both halves are in hand and it costs nobody gas. What
this file buys is that the comparison stays checkable by anyone, indefinitely, with one call —
which before this change it was not.

The boundary, stated rather than discovered: a malformed report can record a lien naming an
address that asked for nothing, and the pool would pay it. Nothing on chain refuses. What is now
true is that anybody can prove it happened, long after the logs are gone.

If that is to be enforced on chain rather than audited, the right place is the registry, which is
what writes the lien — not the pool. It would cost a fifth constructor argument and a mandatory
deployment order, and it would refuse the false lien outright rather than merely refusing to pay
it. Putting the same guard in the pool, which is where it was first drafted, is the worst of the
three: it arrives too late, it does not prevent the false lien, and it charges a cold read on
every disbursement forever.

--- the link that time destroys -------------------------------------------------

A submitter used to exist only inside the ClaimSubmitted log. A full node retains receipts for
10,064 blocks, which at half a second a block is about 84 minutes, after which a query returns an
empty array rather than an error. The stored mapping is the reason the borrower/submitter link
can still be checked a week later, by anyone, with one call.

--- what preemptive poisoning costs, measured ------------------------------------

A hostile submission is an ordinary submission: the cheapest one, measured by receipt against an
Osaka node at the 20 Gwei floor, is 47,192 gas. Arc denominates fees in USDC at eighteen native
decimals, so the cost needs no price assumption:

    47,192 gas x 2e-8  =  $0.00094 per hostile submission

The dossier claimed this attack was closed by binding the lien atomically to its disbursement,
so that an adversary would have to fund the advance — to pay their victim. That construction is
not available: the registry's only write path carries no disbursement, and wiring it to the
money would require the registry to know the pool, a deployment cycle, and a setter, which is
the governance the registry exists not to have.

So the attack is priced, not closed, and the price is small. What raises it is elsewhere and not
yet built: an envelope whose plaintext names the address entitled to submit it, so that copying
someone's ciphertext stops working; and a debtor confirmation that binds the CREDITOR and not
only the invoice. As written, debtor confirmation asks the debtor whether they owe the money,
and a copyist is asserting a real invoice the debtor will truthfully confirm.

One leg of the original argument does hold, and it is the one nobody expected: a poisoner cannot
reach a victim's lien id by shifting characters between components. The seven-digest tuple with
its per-component domain separation closes that, and it is measured elsewhere.
