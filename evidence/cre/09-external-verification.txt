Three outbound calls from inside the enclave, and four booleans out.

The claim under test is the sealed submission already on chain: tx
0xb5c78ba8e089d3c7663f7b1eec034f4b3d094270e87379dab26c5232b54b4b88, whose plaintext is the seven
submitted fields as JSON. The handler opens it, decodes it, and spends three HTTP calls against
two live services.

  binary hash   82168bced6310ff6a4dd2041a83676f5beb4396417298fea9d0121cec95fc6e6
  config hash   25c82d50150aed8b5a1cdc6fd512c376353cce6ffa1a5aaca065a28f5020c785


--- the three calls, in order --------------------------------------------------

  1. POST identity.xero.com/connect/token      Basic, client_credentials, invoices.read
  2. GET  api.xero.com/api.xro/2.0/Invoices    ?where=InvoiceNumber=="ORC1043", Bearer + tenant
  3. GET  data.trade.gov/.../v1/search         ?name=<Contact.Name>&size=3, subscription key

The second depends on the first, so they cannot be issued together. The tenant identifier is
configuration, not a fourth call: resolving it at runtime would cost the margin the collision
check needs.

Nothing else crosses. In particular the plaintext's length is gone: it was in the return value
while nothing confidential distinguished one claim from another, and the ledger's answer changed
that. A length is the body too.

`matches` is the half that was missing. Without it the enclave attests that *an* invoice exists
under that number, not that the claimed one does, and a submitter could pledge a real number
against someone else's amount and due date.


--- the screening is a real answer, not a 200 ----------------------------------

A clean name and a rejected key both return 200 with no matches, so a green arm alone proves
nothing. Two consecutive runs, same subscription key, same binary shape, differing only in the
name screened:

  Contact.Name (Bayside Club)   → screened = true     no match
  "Bank of Kunlun Co Ltd"       → screened = false    match

The second arm was produced by temporarily substituting the name in the source; the file was
restored and rebuilt to the identical hash 82168bce… above. It is an ablation across two runs,
not one run — stated plainly because the divergence is the evidence and the method is part of it.

The name screened comes from the ledger, not from the claim. The claim's identity field is
canonical text, and measured against this same service canonicalisation destroys the query —
three sanctioned entities of more than one word, `total > 0` raw and `total: 0` canonicalised.


--- what the plan predicted, and what the measurement said ---------------------

Two questions were left open, with an inference attached to each. Both inferences were wrong,
and the second was wrong in the safe direction only by luck.

1. Does the simulator enforce `HTTPAction.CallLimit`? The prediction was no, reasoning from the
   secret-ring finding that eight secret reads passed under production limits. It does:

     --limits with HTTPAction.CallLimit = 2
     ✗ [101]LimitExceeded: capability call limit exceeded for http-actions.SendRequest:
       PerWorkflow.HTTPAction.CallLimit ... cannot use 3, limit is 2

   The error names the call count. Three is therefore measured, not asserted — and the budget is
   a live gate locally, not a number waiting for deployment to test it.

2. What happens past the response cap — truncation or error? The inference was error, from the
   existence of `LimitExceeded = 101` and two strings in the CLI binary. It is an error, but a
   different one:

     --limits with HTTPAction.ResponseSizeLimit = 100b
     ✗ [8]ResourceExhausted: HTTP response body of 1083 bytes exceeds the simulation limit of
       100 bytes. This limit mirrors a production constraint.

   Nothing is truncated and nothing is silently short. A handler cannot read a partial body and
   conclude the wrong thing.


--- the bytes, measured from inside ---------------------------------------------

The ResourceExhausted message names the offending body's exact size, so walking the cap upward
sizes each response from inside the enclave rather than from a probe outside it:

  cap    outcome                                       what it establishes
  100b   fails naming 1083 bytes                       token response = 1,083 B
  1083b  passes the token, fails naming 1564 bytes     invoice response = 1,564 B
  1564b  all three pass                                screening response ≤ 1,564 B
  2000b  all three pass

  response                 measured      budget      source of the budget
  token                     1,083 B      —           no key; it is the smallest of the three
  invoice (collection)      1,564 B      2,048 B     data.accounting.invoiceQueryResponseBytes
  screening (clean name)   ≤ 1,564 B     32,768 B    data.screening.responseBytesAtSizeThree

The invoice figure agrees to the byte with the 1,564 B measured externally by the data-source
probe against the same query — two paths to the same number.

The screening figure is a bound, not a pin, and the reason is structural: the invoice response is
1,564 B and arrives first, so no cap the run survives can be tighter than that. The budget it is
checked against is the largest observed over four names at size=3 — 16,305 B for `international`,
where size follows the fatness of the three returned records rather than the total.

The comparison is strictly greater: a body of exactly 1,083 B passed a 1,083 B cap.


--- what this run does not prove ------------------------------------------------

The simulator says so itself, in its own banner: *"The simulator is not a real TEE, and is meant
to debug."* Three specific gaps:

  · Not a real enclave. The code path is real and the calls are real; the attestation is not.
  · The secrets did not come from the vault. `cre workflow simulate` resolves them from the `.env`
    file on disk through its own `-e` flag and never consults the vault — established by four
    experiments in the secret-ring evidence. The vault upload is required for deployment and
    invisible to every local verification.
  · No DON signature and no report. Nothing here is attested by anyone.

What it does prove is that three chained calls against two live services fit inside the enclave's
budget, that the answers are read correctly, and that of roughly 4.2 KB of confidential response
body not one byte reaches the return value.


--- limitations that belong to the system, not to this run ----------------------

The watchlist is a United States consolidation — Treasury, Commerce and State. There are no EU,
UK or UN designations in it, so a counterparty listed only in Europe screens clean.

The name screened is the one the issuing business typed into its own ledger. Screening it is
better than screening the claim's canonical field, but anyone wishing to evade the list can
misspell their own debtor. The registry proves uniqueness, not existence.

The debtor has not agreed to anything at this point. The verdict rests on the ledger and the list
alone; the signed confirmation is collected before submission and travels inside the envelope,
which is why there is no second stage waiting on a human inside the execution timeout.

The ledger organisation is a demo company the provider documents as reset every 28 days — stated
by their documentation, never observed here. The two invoices were seeded 2026-09-10. After a
reset the invoice number changes, because the provider assigns it; nothing outside the corpus
depends on `ORC1043`, which is what keeps re-seeding cheap.
