The enclave decides, encodes ten fields, and crosses the door once.

  binary hash   0b1f29887f50b5bf80e364e566baef2097c9247c74fbd9fcb7b6e87cc7e1098f
  registry      0xf2de8798750ea3bcb0faca049fc7581a118ef1f6


--- the decision, and the six facts that produced it ------------------------------

Two real submissions already on chain, run through the whole handler:

  0x6d004376…61b3   authorized ✓ exists ✓ unpaid ✓ matches ✓ screened ✓ clear ✓ confirmed ✓
                    → kind 1, Record

  0x6b42da69…caab   the same six, and confirmed ✗ — the debtor named a third party as creditor
                    → kind 4, Reject, reason 2 (DebtorUnconfirmed)

The tenth field of the return value is the decision. Everything upstream of it is unchanged, which
is what makes the difference attributable to the one fact that moved.

`undecidable` never approves. A registry that could not answer and a registry that said no are not
the same thing, but neither of them records — collapsing the first into "clear" is how a debtor
with too many liens becomes the easiest one to double-pledge.


--- two implementations, one committed vector --------------------------------------

The Solidity tests that already existed encode in Solidity and decode in Solidity. They cannot
catch a TypeScript encoder that drifts from the schema, and a drifted encoder does not error — it
produces bytes the registry decodes into a *different lien*.

So the vector is emitted once from Solidity, committed to `claim/fixtures/report.json`, and the
TypeScript encoder is checked against it byte for byte:

  record body    576 B    budget 5,011 B    11.5%
  reject body    352 B

Then the same bytes are sent back the other way — through the forwarder, into the real registry:

  test_Registry_RecordsFromTheEncodersOwnBytes       LienRecorded(0x11e, 0xb0110e1, 1799000000)
  test_Registry_RejectsWithTheReasonTheEncoderChose  SubmissionRejected(0x5ab, 6)

The reject arm carries its own nonce. The registry marks a nonce used *before* it looks at the
kind, so reusing the record's body reverts as a replay instead of rejecting — this plan's own
lesson, applied to its own fixture.

Integers travel through the fixture as strings. `chainSelector` is 3,034,092,155,422,581,607, past
`Number.MAX_SAFE_INTEGER`: `JSON.parse` rounds it to 3,034,092,155,422,582,000 and the encoder
would produce a different body without complaining.


--- the budget was left unmeasured by the plan, and it is measurable ---------------

The plan said to declare it unmeasured — that the SDK has no size guard and the simulator was not
known to apply one. It does apply one, and the ablation says something more useful than pass/fail:

  --limits with ChainWrite.EVM.ReportSizeLimit = 500
  ✗ [8]ResourceExhausted: EVM chain write report of 685 bytes exceeds the simulation limit of 500

  cap 684 → refused        cap 685 → accepted

685 against a 576-byte body. The difference is 109 bytes, and 109 is exactly the forwarder header
the body budget is derived from: 5,120 − 109 = 5,011. That arithmetic was taken from the frozen
interface; it is now confirmed from the other side, by the limiter itself, against a real body.

So the number to check a body against is 5,011 — not 5,120, which is the raw limit before the
header, and not 5,000, which is `LogTrigger.EventSizeLimit`, a different limit on a different
object. `budgets.json` now carries all three and says which is which.


--- the nonce, and what it does not protect ----------------------------------------

`keccak256(submissionId ‖ kind)`. Derived, never drawn: the enclave has no entropy and two nodes
disagreeing on a nonce is a report that never reaches consensus.

The kind is in the preimage because the registry remembers every nonce for ever, and the same lien
is later released or defaulted. Without that byte the second of those two reverts as a replay — and
it would be discovered with the workflow already deployed.

What it does not protect against: two copyists submitting the same claim get *different* submission
ids, because the inbox derives one from `keccak(sender ‖ ciphertext)`. Both reports pass the replay
guard, and the second reverts inside the registry with `AlreadyEncumbered`. A revert in `onReport`
emits nothing — no event, no trace, nothing the second submitter can read. The in-enclave collision
check closes that only once the first lien is mined.


--- one crossing -------------------------------------------------------------------

Exactly one `usingTheDons()` and one `.report(` in the whole package, pinned by a test that sweeps
every non-test file in it rather than naming them. The hand-written list this replaced named two
files; three enclave modules had appeared since, the newest of them the one where someone would put
a trace to see what is being signed.

The door is not a filter. Nothing in the SDK stops a payload carrying whatever it likes — what
crosses is exactly what the encoder puts in it. Ten derived fields, and the commitments are
peppered hashes whose pepper stays behind. That is why it is a test and not a claim.

The borrower is `claim.submitter`, from the event topic, never from the plaintext. A copyist
relaying somebody else's ciphertext lands the lien on their own address.


--- what this does not prove -------------------------------------------------------

**That a report lands.** The golden vector proves the bytes are the ones the contract decodes, and
the test forwarder proves `onReport` accepts them. That the DON signs and the production forwarder
transmits is only visible on deployment, and staging cannot exercise it: it carries a different
workflow name on purpose, the registry checks that name, and there is no setter.

**That the test fixture's identity is production's.** `RegistryFixture` deploys with its own
workflow name. The interop test proves the registry accepts *these bytes*; that it accepts them
under the deployed identity is a separate claim.


--- the policy is chosen, not derived ----------------------------------------------

80% advance and 2% fee come from no measurement. There is no default history, no pricing model and
no data in this repository that could produce them, and there will not be. They are published in
configuration precisely so that is visible — the same call the collision threshold makes. What the
system demonstrates is the mechanism: that the decision happens inside the enclave and only the
conclusion leaves. Not that the numbers are right.

**There is no currency conversion anywhere in this system.** The invoice is in the ledger's
currency and the pool settles in USDC. The advance is a fraction of a configured settlement base,
never of the claim's own amount — assigning the claim's minor units into a six-decimal field would
be a scale error of roughly 8,000× wearing the costume of a policy. The base is capped at what the
pool actually holds: 16 USDC on chain, 10 configured, 8 advanced. Closing this properly needs one
more HTTP call to a price source, which would be the sixth of a budget of five. A design
constraint, not an oversight.

**The fee is low for a mechanical reason.** `repay` credits the whole fee to total assets in one
transaction, so anyone can deposit in the block before and take a proportional share without having
carried the term. At demo size that is cents, but it is real and it scales with the fee.
